Heartbeat through a moving wrist

A watch can read your pulse. Then you start walking.

The sensor on the back of a fitness watch works by shining light into your skin. It works well when you hold still. The moment your arm swings, the reading falls apart — and not randomly. It fails in a specific, predictable way that turns out to be fixable.

Why movement breaks it

Blood absorbs green light. Every heartbeat pushes a little pulse of blood through the vessels under the sensor, so slightly less light bounces back. Measure the returning light about sixty times a second and you get a wave with one bump per beat.

Two things go wrong once the arm moves.

The sensor slides

A shift of one millimetre against the skin changes what the light passes through entirely — different tissue, different vessels, daylight leaking into the gap. These swings are far bigger than the heartbeat they are hiding.

Blood moves on its own

Swinging your arm pushes blood around inside it. That genuinely changes how much blood sits under the sensor, so the sensor reports it faithfully — as though it were a heartbeat.

So a walking signal is not a noisy version of a resting signal. It is a different signal with the heartbeat buried somewhere inside it.

The part that makes it genuinely hard

You might hope to separate the two by speed. Heartbeats are fast, arm swings are slow — filter out the slow stuff and you are done.

That hope does not survive contact with the numbers. A brisk walk swings your arm about once a second and lands your feet about twice a second. A heart working at 90 bpm beats one and a half times a second. The interference sits in exactly the same range as the signal.

Here is a single real eight-second stretch of walking, with a true heart rate of 88 bpm. Three different rhythms are present in the sensor data:

Rhythm found in the signalPer minuteWhere it came from
0.87 per second52Arm swinging
1.50 per second90The actual heartbeat
1.75 per second105Feet hitting the ground

Pick the strongest rhythm and you get 90 bpm, which is very nearly right. But the footstep rhythm reached 95% of the heartbeat's strength. Walk slightly faster and it wins — and the watch will confidently tell you your heart is at 105.

The fix comes from the second sensor. The watch also has an accelerometer, the same chip that knows which way up your phone is. It measures the arm swinging directly. So: a rhythm that shows up in both the light sensor and the motion sensor came from your arm. A rhythm that shows up only in the light sensor came from your heart.

That one sentence is the whole idea. The rest is carefully applying it.

Words that appear from here on

Nine of them, and then the rest of the page is readable.

PPG
Photoplethysmography. The light-based pulse sensor. Photo = light, plethysmo = volume change, graphy = recording. A recording of blood volume changes, measured with light.
BVP
Blood volume pulse — what the wrist device calls its PPG output. Same thing, different label.
ECG
Electrocardiogram. Measures the heart's electrical activity through chest electrodes. Much cleaner than PPG, which is why hospitals use it — and why it works here as the correct answer to check against.
Accelerometer
Written ACC. Measures movement along three directions at once. Here it is the motion reference that tells us which rhythms to distrust.
Hertz (Hz)
Repetitions per second. One heartbeat per second is 1 Hz, which is 60 bpm. To convert: bpm = Hz × 60.
Spectrum
Like the bar display on a stereo showing bass, mid and treble. Feed in eight seconds of wiggly signal and it reports how much of each repetition rate is present. A tall spike at 1.5 Hz means something in there repeats about three times every two seconds.
Window
One eight-second slice of recording. Every estimate is made from one window, and windows start two seconds apart — so they overlap heavily.
MAE
Mean absolute error. The average size of the mistake, ignoring direction. An MAE of 8 bpm means being off by about eight beats per minute on a typical reading.
Motion artifact
The catch-all name for signal that movement put there and the body did not. The thing this whole project is about removing.

Where the data comes from

A published research dataset called PPG-DaLiA. Fifteen people went about a day's activities wearing two devices at once — one that measures what we want to predict from, and one that measures the right answer.

Keeping those two apart is the rule the whole project rests on. The chest strap produces the answer key. If any of it leaked into the inputs, the results would describe a device nobody wears.

What the fifteen people were doing

Not a lab protocol of clean repeated exercises. Roughly two and a half hours each of ordinary activity, which is the point — it is where the hard cases live.

ActivityEight-second windowsAverage heart rateHow much the wrist moves
Total64,697

Notice the imbalance. The easy, still activities — sitting, desk work, lunch, driving — are more than half the data. The hard ones are small: stair climbing is 3,185 windows, under five minutes per person.

That matters more than it looks. A method that handles sitting perfectly and fails completely on stairs still posts a respectable overall average, because stairs is 5% of the total. Which is why every result on this page is broken out per activity rather than reported as one number.

How one reading gets made

The recording is chopped into eight-second windows. Each window produces one heart rate estimate, and each window's correct answer comes from counting heartbeats in the chest signal over those same eight seconds.

Windows start two seconds apart, so neighbouring windows share six of their eight seconds. Two consecutive readings are computed from mostly the same heartbeats.

Why that overlap changes how we test

The usual way to test a model is to hide a random slice of data, train on the rest, and see how it does on the hidden part. That would be cheating here: window 1000 and window 1001 are nearly the same eight seconds, so one lands in training and the other in testing, and the model scores brilliantly by having effectively already seen the answer.

Instead we hide an entire person. Train on fourteen, test on the fifteenth, repeat fifteen times so everyone gets a turn. No shared heartbeats are possible, and it matches the real situation: a watch on a stranger. This is called leave-one-subject-out, and every number below uses it.

Let a model learn when to trust which candidate

Stages 1–4 are hand-written rules, and they get you partway. What came out

Every bar is the average error across all fifteen people, always predicting for someone the model never trained on. Shorter is better.

The two grey bars are reference points that make the rest meaningful. Always guess the average is what you get for doing nothing at all — 17.67 bpm. Published deep network is the best result in the original research paper, from a neural network built specifically for this task — 7.65 bpm. Anything between those two is a real attempt.

The check that matters most

There is a trap hiding in this dataset. Look back at the activity table: the activities with the most wrist movement are also the activities with the highest heart rate. Cycling and stairs average around 120 bpm; sitting averages 61.

So a model could score well by silently learning "lots of arm movement means guess high" — recognising the activity, never reading a pulse at all. It would look like a heart rate monitor and be a step counter.

The way to find out is to take the light sensor away entirely and see how well movement alone does.

Movement alone gets to 14.13 — better than guessing, because activity really does say something about heart rate. But the full method is 5.8 bpm better than that, and that gap can only come from actually reading the pulse. The model is measuring a heartbeat, not counting steps.

Broken out by activity

This is the view the project exists to produce. Red is the hand-written rules, green is the learned model.

The pattern is the argument. On still activities both do fine. As movement increases the rules fall apart — error above 23 bpm on walking, cycling and stairs — while the model roughly halves it.

Two honest details visible in that chart. On sitting the model is worse than the simple rules (4.09 against 2.96): when the pulse is already obvious, a plain "pick the strongest rhythm" is close to unbeatable, and a learned model trained mostly on hard cases adds wobble it does not need. And stairs remains bad at 17.88 — the heart rate climbs fast while the arm motion is at its most chaotic, so neither approach really solves it.

Also worth noticing: cycling has a higher heart rate than walking but a lower error. Hands gripping handlebars move far less than a swinging arm. The interference tracks how much the wrist moves, not how hard you are working.

The result nobody puts on a poster

Same method, same settings, fifteen people. The error ranges from 3.87 bpm to 21.51 bpm — a 5.6× spread.

One person (S5) is five times harder to read than another (S7), and nothing in the recorded information about them explains why. Sensor fit, wrist size, skin properties and how tightly the band was worn all plausibly matter and none were measured.

This is the actual frontier in wearable sensing. The average is a solved-ish problem; the spread is not. A watch that works beautifully for most people and poorly for some is a product problem that no amount of model tuning fixes.

Two things that did not work

Kept in because a page that only lists successes is not telling you much.

A hand-designed feature the model ignored

The central insight is that candidate rhythms close to a motion rhythm are suspect. As a diagnostic that held up strongly: 85% of the rule-based failures on walking sat on a motion rhythm or an echo of one, against 11% of its successes.

So it seemed obvious to hand the model a measurement of exactly that — how far each candidate sits from the nearest motion rhythm. It ranked 15th out of 21 in usefulness. The model preferred the raw ingredients: the candidate rhythms themselves plus plain "how hard is the wrist moving", and worked out the relationship on its own.

The lesson generalises. Hand-computed combinations of things the model already has usually just add a redundant column. Feature engineering earns its place when it adds information the model could not have derived.

A fix for the sitting problem that got rejected

Since the simple rules beat the model when sitting, an obvious repair is to switch between them: use the rules when the wrist is still, the model when it moves. The switch point has to be chosen without looking at the test person, so it was picked using a held-out slice of the training people.

All fifteen rounds chose "never switch". Sitting is 4,517 windows — but desk work, lunch and driving are another 28,736 windows that are also low-movement, and there the model wins by 1.5 to 3.5 bpm. Any switch point low enough to catch sitting also catches those, and the trade is badly negative.

The diagnosis: sitting is not distinctive because the wrist is still. It is distinctive because the pulse is unmistakable. Movement was the wrong thing to measure.

What building this taught me

I came into this comfortable with Python and gradient boosting and knowing nothing about signal processing. The signal processing turned out to be the easy half.

Designing the test was harder than building the model

The model itself is a few dozen lines. Working out how to measure it honestly took most of the effort — noticing that overlapping windows make a random train/test split meaningless, and that the only defensible option is hiding an entire person.

That paid for itself immediately. I tuned the rule-based method on one person and got 9.10 bpm, which felt great. Across all fifteen the same settings gave 14.02 — the first number was optimistic by more than half. The protocol caught me overfitting before it reached the model, which is exactly what it is for.

A result needs a floor and a ceiling to mean anything

"8.34 bpm" on its own is unreadable. It only becomes a result next to 17.67 (do nothing at all) and 7.65 (the best published attempt). I now build that comparison before running the model rather than after, because it also works as a tripwire: failing to beat the do-nothing floor means something is broken, not merely mediocre.

The ablation is what makes a number trustworthy

Movement and heart rate are tangled together in this data — people move more when their heart is working harder. So a model could score respectably by recognising the activity and never reading a pulse. Taking the light sensor away and finding that movement alone only reaches 14.13 is the single most important number on this page, and it is not the headline one.

Anyone can report an accuracy figure. Showing that it means what it appears to mean is a different piece of work.

Change one thing at a time, or learn nothing

I added two improvements to the rule-based method at once. The overall error moved by 0.02 bpm — apparently nothing happened. Broken out by activity, cycling had improved 60% and walking had degraded 77%; the two effects had cancelled. I could not tell which change caused which, and had to run both separately to find out.

Plotting it showed why: one of the changes could trap the estimate on a wrong value and hold it there for three minutes. A prior strong enough to help is strong enough to trap.

A good diagnostic is not automatically a good fix

Checking whether failures sat on a motion rhythm separated cleanly — 85% of failures against 11% of successes. That looked like a solved problem. But I had measured it only on the windows that were already failing. Applied to all 64,697 windows, the correction taxed the easy majority to help the hard minority, and the overall error got slightly worse.

Something that discriminates well on the cases you studied can still be a bad intervention on the whole population. Worth checking rather than assuming.

Averages hide the thing you are trying to fix

Stair climbing is 5% of this dataset. A method could fail completely on stairs and still post a decent overall average — which means the overall average is the wrong way to watch the problem this project is about. Breaking results out by activity and by person is where both the interesting result (error halves under movement) and the uncomfortable one (a 5.6× spread between people) actually live.

Why these particular lessons

I want to work on health and fitness sensing, and the recurring problem in that field is not model architecture. It is that a device has to work on a stranger's wrist, in conditions nobody controlled, and that the gap between a demo and a product is mostly about honest measurement — knowing when your signal is unusable, knowing which population your numbers apply to, and being able to say what your method does not do.

The two failed experiments on this page took about as long as the successful ones, and I think they say more about whether I would be useful on a team. Both of them are cases where the obvious idea was wrong and the way to find out was to test it properly rather than reason about it harder.
